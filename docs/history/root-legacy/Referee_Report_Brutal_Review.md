# Referee Report — finfluencer_tr_2025_BIR_Methods_Results_FINAL_with_discussion.docx

Read in full: title, Introduction, Conceptual Framework (2.1–2.8), Method (3.1–3.9), Results (4.1–4.8), Discussion (5.1–5.3), Conclusion, References. This is written the way I would actually score it for BIR, not softened.

## Verdict up front

The empirical core is solid — real data, real numbers, honest hedging on causality, sensible robustness checks. But the manuscript as it currently stands is **not submittable**. There is a fatal, immediately visible defect (Section 5.3), and a second-tier problem (corrupted citations) that would draw a formal misconduct query from a diligent editor, not just a revise-and-resubmit. Both are mechanical and fixable in under an hour; neither reflects badly on the underlying research. But if this goes out the door as-is, it gets desk-rejected on page 8 before a reviewer even reaches the Results.

## 1. Desk-reject-level defect

**AI editing changelog left inside the manuscript body.** Immediately after Section 5.3 (Limitations and Future Research) and before "6. Conclusion," six paragraphs of pure meta-commentary appear as normal manuscript text:

> "Replaced the literature-restating opening with a contribution-led framing that preserves both the central message-messenger claim and the secondary, confirmatory status of the market-integration extension."
> "Converted the empirical-style result recap into a theoretical interpretation paragraph, eliminating all numerical repetition."
> "Added explicit subsections for theoretical contribution, practical implications, limitations, and future research, in the order requested, populated only with material already present in the manuscript."
> ...and three more in the same voice.

This is a changelog describing edits to the Discussion section — clearly generated by whatever tool last touched this file — sitting in the manuscript as if it were prose, styled identically to the surrounding paragraphs. Any editor or reviewer hits this within the first read-through. It reads as either gross carelessness or, worse, invites the question of how much of the rest of the document went through the same unsupervised process without a final human read. **This must be deleted before anything else about this paper matters.**

## 2. Reference corruption — real, not cosmetic

The References section was clearly regenerated by a citation manager (EndNote paragraph style is still tagged on every entry) at some point after the prose was finalized, and the regeneration broke names that a specialist reviewer in this exact literature will recognize on sight.

- **"Long, J. B. D., Shleifer, A., Summers, L. H., & Waldmann, R. J. (1990)"** — this is De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann's *Noise Trader Risk in Financial Markets*, one of the most cited papers in the field. The parser split "De Long" into "Long, J. B. D.," dropping "De" as if it were a middle initial. Every in-text citation throughout the paper (Introduction, Conceptual Framework, Discussion) now reads "Long et al. (1990)" instead of "De Long et al. (1990)." This is the kind of error that signals to a reviewer the reference list was never proofread by a human who knows the literature.
- **"Horton, D., & Richard Wohl, R. (1956)"** — same failure mode on R. Richard Wohl's name; the reference list has it backwards ("Richard Wohl, R." instead of "Wohl, R. R."), and the in-text citation reads "Horton and Richard Wohl (1956)" throughout Section 2.4 — awkward and wrong.
- **Hovland, Janis, & Kelley (1953)** reference entry is truncated to "Communication and persuasion." with no subtitle and no publisher (should be *Communication and persuasion: Psychological studies of opinion change*, Yale University Press).
- **A citation is misattributed to the wrong source.** Section 2.5 attributes two separate findings to "Tversky and Kahneman (1974)": the heuristics-and-biases result (correct) and "resulting choices depart from expected-utility predictions in framing-sensitive ways" (incorrect — that is Kahneman & Tversky's 1979 Prospect Theory finding, published in Econometrica). The 1979 paper is cited nowhere and does not appear in the reference list at all. This isn't a formatting slip; it's a real finding attributed to the wrong publication.
- **Two citations were silently swapped for different real papers**, not fabricated, but the substitution changes what's being claimed and was never re-verified against the sentence it supports:
  - Media richness/social presence is now attributed to Daft & Lengel (1986) *and* Fortunati & Manganelli (2008) — the second is a real 2008 *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* paper on social representations of telecommunications, but it is not the classical social-presence citation (Short, Williams, & Christie, 1976) the argument was originally built on, and no one appears to have checked that Fortunati & Manganelli's actual claims support the sentence they're now attached to.
  - Herding/informational cascades is now "Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, & Welch (2018)," a real 2018 Palgrave Dictionary of Economics entry by the same three authors — but it is not their famous 1992 *Journal of Political Economy* paper, which is what "informational cascades" conventionally refers to in this literature. Citing the encyclopedia entry instead of the original theoretical paper is a defensible but unusual choice that a reviewer will question.
  - The two-step-flow citation is now "Katz, Lazarsfeld, & Roper (2017)" — a real Routledge reissue of *Personal Influence*, but every other paper in this literature (including this manuscript's own earlier drafts) cites the 1955 original. Citing the reprint is fine in isolation; doing it inconsistently with the surrounding literature is not.

None of these five are fabricated citations — that's the one piece of good news, and a real improvement over an earlier draft of this Introduction, which did contain unverifiable citations that are correctly absent here. But "not fabricated" is a low bar. Right now the reference list would fail a five-minute spot check by any coauthor who has read the papers being cited.

## 3. Internal-consistency error a reviewer will catch immediately

Section 4.7 opens: **"Table 7 summarises seven robustness checks."** It then walks through exactly four ("First... Second... Third... Fourth...") and closes with **"Across all four checks, the central finding... is confirmed."** The section cannot decide whether it ran four checks or seven. If three checks exist but weren't written up, that's a bigger problem than a typo — it means the manuscript claims robustness testing it doesn't show its work for. This needs a definitive fix, not a find-and-replace of "seven" to "four," until you've confirmed which is actually true against the underlying analysis outputs.

## 4. Substantive methodological pressure points (not fatal, but a reviewer will lean on all of these)

- **The one "robust" finding is the one analyst with the most concentrated topic mix.** Section 4.7's own HHI robustness check shows Şatıroğlu is meaningfully more topic-concentrated (HHI=0.129) than the other three analysts (0.053–0.077) — and Şatıroğlu is also the *only* analyst whose messenger effect survives all three estimation methods. The paper's own Section 2.6 flags, as a theoretical limitation, that 128 discrete topics may not fully separate within-topic framing from source identity. It never comes back empirically to rule out that Şatıroğlu's "robust messenger effect" is partly a residual topic-selection effect that a coarser or finer topic grain would attenuate. A reviewer in this subfield will ask for a specification that interacts topic granularity with the Şatıroğlu contrast specifically, not just an omnibus robustness table.
- **The central "messenger effect" claim generalizes to exactly one of four analysts.** Geçer is significant under naive and mixed-model specs but not cluster-robust SEs; Yeşilada is significant under naive and cluster-robust but not the mixed model. Only Şatıroğlu holds up everywhere. The Abstract and Conclusion describe "communicator identity" as "statistically significant" in general terms that a careful reader will find overstated relative to what Table 7 actually shows. This should be qualified explicitly wherever the messenger effect is asserted outside the Şatıroğlu-specific claim, not just in the robustness table's fine print.
- **Yeşilada's subsample is extremely thin** — 8 videos, 510 comments, versus 93–99 videos and 3,600–8,200 comments for the other three. Every cross-analyst comparison involving Yeşilada is comparing a well-powered estimate against a barely-powered one. This is disclosed but under-weighted in how confidently Yeşilada-involving contrasts are discussed in the text (e.g., "Başaran–Yeşilada, p=.013" is reported as a clean significant pairwise contrast in 4.2 with no caveat about the underlying sample asymmetry).
- **The sentiment classifier is a general-domain Turkish BERT model, not finance-adapted**, validated on only 462 gold binary labels (MCC=0.719 — respectable, not outstanding) and forced into a binary positive/negative frame with no true neutral class. The 6.8% "pseudo-neutral" band is flagged but never analyzed for whether it behaves differently in the main results — it's acknowledged and then dropped.
- **Figure 2's own caption undercuts the "fully reproducible pipeline" claim in the Introduction's fifth contribution**: the caption states the canonical BERTopic intertopic-distance map could not be produced because "the cached embedding index required for the latter was unavailable at analysis time." If a required artifact for a headline figure isn't reproducible on demand, that's worth a sentence in Limitations, not just a figure footnote — and it sits awkwardly next to a contributions paragraph that explicitly claims full reproducibility.
- **128 topics from 17,566 comments (~137 comments/topic on average, with several reported topics at n=33–52) is a lot of topics for this corpus size.** Several of the headline effect sizes in Table 3 are drawn from cells small enough that FDR correction controls the false-discovery rate but doesn't rescue the precision of the individual Cohen's h estimates. Worth reporting confidence intervals on the Table 3 effect sizes directly in text, not just the point estimates.

## 5. What the paper does well (a fair reviewer says this too)

The Granger-causality framing is genuinely disciplined — "temporal predictability, not economic causality" is stated and re-stated correctly every single time it comes up, including in the Conclusion, which is rarer than it should be in this literature. The market-integration section is honestly scoped as confirmatory and secondary throughout, never oversold. The human-validation protocol (blinded coders, third-party adjudication, bootstrapped CIs, pre-registered κ threshold) is more rigorous than most sentiment-analysis papers in finance journals bother with. The variance-decomposition logic (topic vs. analyst, sequential partial R², cross-validated against mixed-model LR tests) is the right design for the research question and is executed correctly as far as the numbers presented are internally check.

6. Add an explicit qualifier wherever "communicator identity is significant" is asserted in the Abstract/Discussion/Conclusion that this holds robustly for one of four analysts, not all four.
